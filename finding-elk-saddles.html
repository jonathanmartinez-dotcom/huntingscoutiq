Lesson 02 · Terrain

What are saddles?

One of the most predictable travel corridors in elk country.

Elk do not cross mountains at random. They cross at the low, easy points — and once you learn to spot those, you can predict where elk naturally travel before you ever set foot on the mountain.

Video lesson coming soon

01What is a saddle?

A saddle is the low point on a ridgeline between two higher peaks — the spot where, instead of climbing straight over the top, the terrain dips down and gives you an easier way across. Think of it as a mountain pass. Animals, and people, naturally choose the easiest crossing.

Peak

The highest point.

Saddle

The lowest point between two higher elevations.

Drainage

Terrain falls away on both sides.

Travel corridor

The natural crossing point through the ridge.

02Why elk use saddles

The saddle advantage
✓Easier travel. Less climbing than going up and over the ridge.
✓Connects drainages. A saddle links feeding and bedding areas in adjacent basins.
✓Wind advantage. Airflow funnels through the gap, giving elk a steady read on danger.
✓Predictable movement. Multiple animals naturally use the same crossing, again and again.
✓Hunting opportunity. All that movement gets concentrated into one narrow spot.

Elk don't cross mountains randomly.
Saddles naturally funnel movement.

03How to spot a saddle

Four cues on the map
1Between two peaks. Look for the lowest point on a ridgeline.
2Hourglass contours. The contour lines pinch together, then open back up.
3Connects two basins. Terrain drops away on both sides of the gap.
4Easy crossing. Picture the path that requires the least climbing — that is the one elk pick.

Try it: find three saddles in your unit before your next hunt.

04Putting it together

Here is the part that matters — and it is the same lesson as the benches. A saddle by itself is just a low spot. It becomes powerful when multiple resources intersect nearby.

What to look for around the saddle
Saddle — the primary crossing.
Bedding timber — north-facing cover nearby.
Water — a creek in the drainage below.
Wind — how the morning thermals move through the gap.
Access route — how you approach without blowing it out.
Elk travel — the line of movement through the saddle.

Read the mountain. Connect the clues. Find elk.
