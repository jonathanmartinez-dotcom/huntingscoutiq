Why benches matter — and why not every bench holds elk

One of the biggest mistakes hunters make is finding one terrain feature they heard elk like — and assuming that is where all the elk are.

You have probably heard someone say "find a bench." That is good advice. But it is incomplete — because not every bench holds elk. Here is what a bench actually is, why elk use them, and what separates an average bench from one that is worth hunting.

01What a bench actually is

A bench is simply a relatively flat shelf on the side of a mountain. Instead of climbing straight up or dropping straight down, the terrain levels out for a short distance before continuing. On a topo map you will see the contour lines spread farther apart than the terrain around them — that is your first clue. Benches can be tiny, just a few yards wide, or stretch hundreds of yards across an entire mountainside.

02Why elk like them: energy

The biggest reason is simple — energy. Everything an elk does costs calories. Walking straight up a mountain burns a lot of them; walking across a relatively flat bench does not. If an elk can cover the same distance while burning less energy, that is usually the route it will choose. Nature is incredibly efficient.

03Benches are travel corridors

But benches are not just easy walking — think of them as highways. Elk use them to move from one destination to another: leaving a bedding area to feed, dropping toward water, traveling between two drainages. A bench often becomes the easiest path through otherwise steep terrain.

04Sometimes bedding — but not always

Here is where a lot of people stop. They think bench equals bedding. Sometimes that is true. But not always. Elk do not bed just because the ground is flat — they bed where multiple conditions come together.

05What makes a good bench?

Compare two benches.

An average bench
  • ✗ Completely open, no trees
  • ✗ No shade
  • ✗ No water nearby
  • ✗ No escape cover
Still a bench. But why would an elk spend the day there?
A bench worth hunting
  • ✓ Surrounded by dark timber
  • ✓ A spring a few hundred yards away
  • ✓ An open feeding meadow just below
  • ✓ Predictable wind — they hear danger coming
  • ✓ Over the ridge and gone in seconds
That is an entirely different bench.

06The variables that matter

This is where scouting gets interesting. A bench is never evaluated by itself. Instead, ask:

Questions I ask about every bench
· Does it have bedding cover?
· How close is food?
· Where is the nearest water?
· How hard is hunter access?
· What is the prevailing wind?
· How do thermals move here?
· North-facing or south-facing?
· What is the canopy cover?
· How steep are the escape routes?
· Right elevation for the season?
· Has it burned recently?

Every one of those changes how attractive that bench becomes.

Don't hunt terrain features.
Hunt terrain relationships.

The one thing to take from this lesson

The bench itself is not the destination. It is how that bench connects food, cover, water, security, wind, and movement that matters.

07How I scout

When I look at satellite imagery, I do not start by asking "where is the best bench?" I start by asking "where would an elk want to spend an entire day?" The bench is just one piece of that answer. Once I find a promising one, I zoom out and look at the entire basin — how does it connect to everything else? Because elk do not live on a single point. They live in a landscape.

08The takeaway

So yes — benches are incredibly important. But they are just one variable among many. The goal is not to memorize a list of terrain features; it is to understand why elk choose one place over another. That is what makes scouting fun.
